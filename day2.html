Section 3 · Research Without Relying on AI

Build your own research process instead of asking a chatbot for the answer.

Why do it yourself?

AI tools can summarize things quickly, but they can also get facts wrong, present outdated information as current, or miss context — with no easy way for you to tell which parts to trust. Doing your own research builds a skill AI can't replace: the ability to find, weigh, and connect information yourself. It's also the only way to verify what an AI (or anyone else) tells you.

The research loop

① Ask a clear question

"How does recycling plastic actually work?" beats "tell me about plastic."

② Brainstorm keywords

List synonyms and related terms: recycling, plastic sorting, resin codes, PET, circular economy.

③ Search broad, then narrow

Start general, skim what comes back, then refine your query with what you learn.

④ Skim before you read

Scan titles, snippets, and headers to find the 2-3 pages worth reading closely.

⑤ Take notes with sources

Record the fact, the source, and the date — you'll need it later to double-check or cite.

⑥ Cross-check

Confirm key facts against a second, independent source before treating them as true.

Diversify where you look

Relying on a single article — or a single type of source — narrows your view. Mix it up:

Source typeGood for
News organizationsRecent events, on-the-ground reporting
Government (.gov)Official data, statistics, regulations
Universities (.edu) / academic journalsIn-depth research, studies, expert analysis
Nonprofits & organizations (.org)Specialized knowledge — but check for bias/agenda
Primary sourcesOriginal documents, raw data, first-hand accounts
Watch for confirmation bias It's tempting to stop researching the moment you find a source that agrees with what you already believed. Deliberately look for at least one source that challenges or complicates your initial view.

Section 4 · Finding Credible Sources

Tools to judge whether a source deserves your trust.

The CRAAP test

A quick five-point check before you rely on a source:

C — Currency

When was it published or last updated? Is that recent enough for your topic?

R — Relevance

Does it actually address your question, at the right depth for your needs?

A — Authority

Who wrote or published it? What are their credentials or expertise?

A — Accuracy

Is it supported by evidence and citations? Can you verify the claims elsewhere?

P — Purpose

Why does this source exist — to inform, sell, persuade, or entertain? Does that shade the content?

Lateral reading

Professional fact-checkers rarely dig deep into a suspicious page itself first. Instead, they leave it — opening a new tab and searching the publication's name or author's name to see what others say about their track record. This is called lateral reading, and it's faster and more reliable than staring harder at the original page.

Try it Before trusting a claim from an unfamiliar site, search: "[site name]" reliable OR bias OR fact-check in a new tab. See what independent reviewers and other outlets say.

Red flags to watch for

Signs of credibility

  • Named author with visible credentials
  • Sources and links to evidence
  • Corrections policy / editorial standards
  • Matches independent reporting elsewhere

Warning signs

  • No author, or a vague "staff" byline
  • Heavy emotional or alarmist language
  • No sources, data, or citations at all
  • Contradicts multiple other reputable outlets

When a claim really matters, cross-check it with an independent fact-checking organization (for example Reuters Fact Check, AP Fact Check, or FactCheck.org) before sharing or relying on it.

Section 5 · Wikipedia & Online Encyclopedias

Use them as a smart starting point, not the final word.

What Wikipedia actually is

Wikipedia is a free, collaboratively edited online encyclopedia — anyone can propose an edit, and a community of volunteer editors reviews changes. That structure gives it huge breadth and speed (articles on almost any topic, updated within hours of breaking news) but also means quality varies: some pages are meticulously sourced, others are thin or occasionally vandalized before it's caught.

How to use it like a researcher

📖 Read the lead first

The intro paragraph summarizes the whole article — perfect for a fast overview before you go deeper.

🔗 Follow the citations

Scroll to "References" and click through to the original sources. That's often the real research goldmine.

💬 Check the Talk page

Every article has a "Talk" tab showing debates among editors — useful for spotting controversial or disputed claims.

🕓 Check the history

The "View history" tab shows how often and recently a page was edited — a very fresh, unstable page deserves extra caution.

Quality markers A small bronze star means "Featured Article" (Wikipedia's highest quality bar); a green plus icon means "Good Article." Neither guarantees perfection, but both indicate the page passed community review.
The golden rule Treat Wikipedia as a launchpad, not a citable authority for serious work: use it to get oriented and to find the primary sources in its reference list, then verify the facts you actually need against those original sources.
