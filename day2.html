💡 Mission 3: Go Full Detective

Leo wants to just ask a chatbot. Maya's got a better idea — solve the case yourselves.
About 35 min
💡 An idea sparks to life

Why find out for yourself?

Imagine learning to ride a bike. Someone could carry you everywhere instead — it would be faster at first. But you'd never actually learn to ride. Asking a chatbot for a fast answer is a bit like that. It can be quick, but it can also get things wrong, or mix up old information with new. When you find an answer yourself, you build a skill that stays with you: knowing how to think something through, compare ideas, and explain why it's true.

Six easy steps

① Ask a clear question

"How does recycling plastic work?" is better than "tell me about plastic," because it points you toward exactly what you want to know.

② Think of a few keywords

Recycling, plastic sorting, reusing plastic — a few different words help you find more useful pages.

③ Search broad, then narrow

Start general and see what comes up. Then search again using new words you just learned.

④ Skim before you read

Look at titles and short previews first. Pick 2–3 pages that seem worth reading closely.

⑤ Write down what you learn

Jot down the fact and where it came from. You'll want that later, especially if you need to double-check it.

⑥ Check it in a second place

Find the same fact somewhere else before you fully trust it. Two matching sources is a great sign.

Let's try it: How do bees make honey? Step 1–2: Our question is clear, and good keywords are "bees," "honey," and "how it's made." Step 3: A broad search for how bees make honey brings back lots of results. Step 4: We skim three titles and pick two that look clear and simple. Step 5: We write down: "Bees collect nectar from flowers, then turn it into honey inside the hive." Step 6: Both pages say almost the same thing, so we can trust it. Done!

Effective ways to find better results

Sometimes your first search doesn't get you very far. Real researchers have a toolkit of tricks for when that happens:

🔄 Try a synonym

If "recycling plastic" doesn't work well, try "reusing plastic" or "plastic waste" instead. Different words unlock different pages.

❓ Turn your question into keywords

"Why do leaves change color in fall?" becomes leaves change color fall — pull out the important words only.

🎯 Get more specific if there's too much

Too many results about "space"? Try "space exploration history" or "Mars rover facts" to zoom in.

🌍 Get more general if there's too little

No results for a very specific question? Back up to a broader topic and work your way in from there.

More ways to research beyond a search engine

A search engine is a great starting point, but it's not the only tool. Real researchers use many different ways to learn:

🙋 Ask an expert

A teacher, librarian, or trusted adult often knows a topic deeply, or can point you toward a great source.

📚 Visit the library

Libraries (and their websites) have carefully chosen books and databases that are often more reliable than a random search result.

🎬 Watch a trusted documentary

A well-made documentary from a museum, science channel, or educational source can teach you a lot in an engaging way.

🔬 Try it yourself

For some questions, a simple safe experiment or observation teaches you more than any article — like watching how ice melts at different temperatures.

What to avoid — and what NOT to skip

Good research is as much about habits to drop as habits to keep. Here's the difference between the two:

Don't skip these

  • Reading more than one source
  • Writing notes in your own words
  • Checking when a source was published
  • Double-checking a surprising claim
  • Asking a trusted adult when you're stuck

Avoid these

  • Trusting only one source
  • Copying text without understanding it
  • Believing a chatbot's answer with no checking
  • Giving up after one confusing search
  • Ignoring the publish date on fast-changing topics
Second try: Do sharks sleep? We search do sharks sleep and find two science sites that both explain that some sharks rest in a low-activity state while still moving water over their gills. Since two independent, careful sources agree, we can write this down with confidence — and we note both sources in case we need to check again later.

Look in more than one place

Reading just one article — or always the same kind of website — only shows you part of the picture. Try mixing it up:

Watch out for this It feels good to find a source that agrees with what you already thought. This feeling has a name: confirmation bias, and everyone experiences it. Try to find one more source too — even one that sees things a little differently. That's how you learn the whole story instead of just the part you expected.
💭 Think about it: You find one website that says something surprising. Should you believe it right away?

Not yet! One surprising claim from one website isn't enough on its own. Search for the same fact somewhere else. If two or three trustworthy sources agree, you can feel much more confident. If nobody else says it, that's a big clue to slow down.

💭 Think about it: Your first search gets zero good results. What should you try next?

Don't give up after one try! Swap in a synonym, turn your question into simpler keywords, or try a broader (or narrower) version of your topic. Real researchers almost always search more than once before finding the right page.

✅ Mission 4: Busted or Trusted?

A wild headline just showed up. Time to find out if it's the real deal — or a total fake.
About 35 min
✅ Stamped: verified!

The CRAAP test

Not every website tells the truth. Some are careful and well-checked. Others are careless, or even trying to trick you on purpose. The CRAAP test is a quick, five-question check that helps you tell the difference. It spells a funny word so it's easy to remember:

C — Currency

When was it made or updated? For fast-changing topics like news, a very old page might be outdated.

R — Relevance

Does it really answer your question, or is it about something only a little bit related?

A — Authority

Who wrote it? Do they actually know the topic well, like a scientist writing about science?

A — Accuracy

Can you find the same facts somewhere else too? Good sources can be double-checked.

P — Purpose

Why does it exist — to calmly inform you, or to sell you something, or to make you feel a strong emotion?

Where to look in the first place

You can save yourself a lot of CRAAP-testing by starting in good neighborhoods online. These are generally solid places to begin:

📰 Established news organizations

Outlets with a long history of careful reporting and named journalists.

🏛️ Government & education sites

Addresses ending in .gov or .edu are usually run by official institutions.

📚 Library databases & encyclopedias

Curated collections that have already been checked by librarians and editors.

⚠️ Be extra careful with social media

Anyone can post anything. Treat social media posts as a starting clue to investigate, not a finished fact.

The "check somewhere else" trick

Real fact-checkers rarely stare harder at a suspicious page to decide if it's true. Instead, they leave the page completely. They open a new tab and search the website's name to see what other people say about it. This is called checking sideways, and professionals use it because it's fast and it works.

Try it Before trusting a claim from a site you don't know, search: "[website name]" reliable OR trustworthy in a new tab and see what shows up. If trusted news sites or fact-checkers have written about that website being unreliable, you'll usually find it quickly.

Spot the difference

🚩 Not credible

"Doctors Are Furious! This One Fruit Cures Everything!!" — no author name, flashing ads, and it wants you to buy a supplement right now. No authority, no accuracy check possible, and its purpose is to sell.

✅ Credible

"New Study Finds Regular Exercise Linked to Better Sleep, Researchers Say" — written by a named health journalist, links to the actual study, and calmly explains what is and isn't yet proven.

Warning signs to watch for

Signs it's trustworthy

  • A real author's name you can look up
  • Links to where the facts came from
  • Calm language, not big emotional claims
  • Matches what other trusted sites say

Warning signs

  • No author listed at all
  • Big, scary, or overly exciting claims
  • No proof or sources given anywhere
  • Asks you to buy or click something right away
💭 Think about it: A page has a real author's name, but it's still trying to sell you something. Is it automatically trustworthy?

Not automatically! A named author is a good sign, but check the "P" in CRAAP too — the purpose. If the page's main goal is to sell you a product, its facts might be shaped to make the product look better than it really is. A real name helps, but it's just one of the five checks.

💭 Think about it: A post on social media has thousands of likes and shares. Does that mean it's true?

No! Something can be very popular and still be completely false — popularity just means lots of people saw and shared it, not that anyone checked it. Treat a viral post as a starting clue, then use the CRAAP test and checking sideways before you believe or share it yourself.

📖 Mission 5: Crack the Vault

A 250-year-old treasure chest of facts is waiting. Let's learn how to open it the smart way.
About 30 min
📖 The vault creaks open

What is Britannica?

Encyclopaedia Britannica is one of the world's most trusted encyclopedias. People have used it for over 250 years! Real experts and editors write and check every article before it's published — often people who have studied that exact topic for years. That's very different from a random website, where anyone at all could have typed anything, with nobody checking it.

How to use it well

🔎 Search your topic

Type what you want to know into Britannica's search bar, just like you would with a search engine.

📖 Read the summary first

The top of the article gives you the big picture in just a few sentences before you read further.

🧑‍🏫 Check who wrote it

Britannica shows you the real expert who wrote or checked the article — look for their name.

🔗 Explore related topics

Follow the "Related Topics" links at the bottom to learn even more about your subject.

Getting even more out of an article

🔍 Search inside the page

Use Ctrl+F (or Cmd+F on a Mac) to jump straight to a word or phrase inside a long article.

📅 Check the last-updated date

Britannica shows when an article was last revised — handy for fast-changing topics.

🧒 Try the kids' version

Britannica also has a version written specifically for younger readers, with simpler language.

📝 Follow the citations

Longer articles often link to the original studies or sources — great for digging even deeper.

Some short articles on Britannica are free for everyone to read. Longer, deeper articles sometimes need a paid subscription — if you're not sure, ask a trusted adult or teacher for help.

Good habit Britannica is a great place to start — but for anything really important, it's still smart to double-check with one more trusted source. That's exactly what real researchers do, even the experts!
Remember Reading an article to learn is great. Copying it word-for-word and turning it in as your own work is not okay — always explain things in your own words. That's how you actually remember what you learned, too.
💭 Think about it: An article has no listed author at all. Should you trust it as much as a Britannica article?

No — treat it more carefully. Britannica always shows a real expert behind each article, which is a strong trust signal. A page with no author at all is missing one of the most important clues you have for judging whether it's trustworthy.

💭 Think about it: Besides Britannica, where else could you look for well-checked information?

Your library's website and databases, museum and .gov educational pages, and other respected encyclopedias are all good options. The key habit isn't picking one perfect source forever — it's knowing how to judge any source using what you've learned in this course.
