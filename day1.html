Section 1 · Staying Safe Online

Passwords, tricky messages, strangers, sneaky software, and simple habits that keep you safe.
About 35 min

Why this matters

You do so many things on a screen. You play games. You do schoolwork. You chat with friends. All of that is great! But just like in real life, a few people online try to trick others. The good news is simple: a few small habits can keep you safe almost all of the time. Let's learn them one at a time.

1. Passwords lock your stuff up tight

Think of a password like a key to your front door. If your key is weak, or if everyone has a copy of it, someone could walk right in. Here is how to make a strong key:

  • Use a different password for every account. If one gets stolen, the others stay safe.
  • Make it long instead of clever. A silly made-up sentence like purple-dragon-eats-tacos is much stronger than Pass123, and it's easier to remember too.
  • Never use your name, birthday, or pet's name. Those are the very first things a trickster will guess.
  • A password manager is a helpful app that remembers all your passwords for you, so you don't have to.
  • Two-factor authentication, or 2FA for short, adds a second lock. Even if someone steals your password, they still can't get in without the second code — usually one sent to your phone.
Common mistake Using "Fluffy2010!" because it feels personal and easy to remember. But if your pet's name and your birth year are on your social media, that's one of the very first things a trickster will try.
💭 Think about it: Your friend tells you their password so you can log in for them. Is that okay?

It's best to say no, even to a friend! A password is just for you. If your friend shares it once, they might have to share it again, and it's harder to keep it safe. You can still help a friend without knowing their password — just show them how to type it in themselves.

2. Spotting tricky messages (phishing)

Phishing is when someone pretends to be a person or company you trust — like a store, a game, or even a friend — so that you'll click a link or give away information. It can show up as a text, an email, or a message inside a game or app. Here are the clues that give it away:

⏱️ It rushes you

"Click right now or you'll lose your account!" Real companies almost never demand instant action like this.

🔗 The link looks odd

Look closely before you click. If the words say one thing, but the actual web address looks strange or misspelled, that's a warning sign.

✉️ The sender looks fake

"amaz0n-security.net" is not really Amazon. Always check the whole address, not just the name that's shown.

🎁 It's too good to be true

A surprise prize, gift card, or free item that asks you to pay first or "verify" your info is almost always a trick.

😱 It scares you

"A virus was found on your device!" or "Someone tried to log in as you!" Fear is a favorite trick to make you click without thinking.

🔠 Odd spelling or grammar

Real companies proofread carefully. Lots of typos or strange phrasing is a common sign of a scam message.

Real message vs. tricky message A real order update usually says something calm, like "Your package will arrive Tuesday." A tricky message tries to create a strong feeling fast — excitement ("You won!"), fear ("Your account is locked!"), or curiosity ("See who viewed your profile!"). When a message makes you feel a big rush of emotion, that's exactly the moment to slow down.

3. Sneaky software: malware & viruses

Malware is short for "malicious software" — a program made to harm your device, steal information, or spy on you. A virus is one type of malware that spreads from file to file, like a cold spreading between people. Malware doesn't usually break in on its own — it tricks you into letting it in.

⬇️ Fake "download" buttons

Some websites are covered in buttons that look like the real download, but actually install unwanted programs. Only click the button that matches exactly what you're looking for.

📎 Surprise attachments

An email attachment you weren't expecting — even from someone you know — can carry a virus if their account was hacked.

🚨 "You have a virus!" pop-ups

A loud pop-up that says to call a number or click to "fix" your device right now is almost always fake. This trick is called scareware.

🎮 Cracked games & free "premium" apps

Free copies of paid games or apps from unofficial sites are one of the most common ways malware sneaks onto a device.

If you ever think a device might have malware, don't try to fix it alone — tell a trusted adult right away so they can help safely.

4. Talking to people you meet online

Games, apps, and websites can connect you with people from all over. Some of them become real friends! But it's smart to remember that you can't always be sure who someone really is on the other side of a screen.

  • Never share your full name, home address, school name, or phone number with someone you only know online.
  • If someone you don't know in real life asks to meet up, or asks you to keep a secret from your family, tell a trusted adult right away.
  • It's okay to block or report anyone who makes you feel uncomfortable — that's exactly what those buttons are for.
  • If a message ever makes you feel confused, worried, or pressured, that's a sign to stop and talk to a trusted adult.
💭 Think about it: Someone from an online game says they're "the same age as you" and asks for your phone number to "stay in touch." What should you do?

Say no, and tell a trusted adult about the message. You can't be sure who is really behind an online account — even someone who seems friendly and claims to be your age. Staying in touch through the game or app itself is safer than sharing personal contact details.

5. Keeping your information private

  • Check your privacy settings so you know who can see your posts, photos, and location.
  • Think before you post. A picture or comment can travel much further than you expect, and it can be very hard to take back.
  • Be careful on public Wi-Fi, like at a café or airport. Avoid typing passwords or private details while connected to it.
  • A padlock icon and "https" in the address bar mean the connection is locked and scrambled — but that doesn't automatically mean the website itself is honest.

Do

  • Use a password manager + 2FA
  • Ask a trusted adult if a message feels weird
  • Keep your apps updated
  • Pause before clicking anything urgent
  • Block or report anyone who makes you uncomfortable

Don't

  • Reuse passwords everywhere
  • Click links in surprise messages
  • Share codes or passwords with anyone
  • Give your address or school name to online strangers
  • Download "free" versions of paid apps or games

How does a search engine even work?

A search engine like Google is a giant, super-organized library. Before you ever type anything, it has already looked through billions of web pages and built a huge list of what's on each one. When you search, it doesn't search the whole internet in that second — it just looks through the list it already built, and shows you the best matches almost instantly.

Use a few words, not a whole sentence

Because Google is matching words on that list, it doesn't need a full question. Instead of typing "what is the best way to remove a coffee stain from a white shirt", just try remove coffee stain white shirt. Fewer words, better results, every time.

Search tricks — your shortcuts

These small symbols help Google understand exactly what you want, so you get better answers with less scrolling:

Try it yourself Looking for a downloadable report about online scams? Try: online scams report filetype:pdf site:.gov. That single search skips blogs and ads, and goes straight to official PDFs.
💭 Think about it: You want a video showing how to tie shoelaces, but not from one certain website. What would you search?

A short, simple search like tie shoelaces video works great — Google already understands you want a video and will show video results near the top. You don't need to add "site:" at all unless you're trying to search inside one specific website.

Quick answers, right in the search box

Google can skip the search results entirely for some questions and just show you the answer:

🧮 Math & calculations

Type 248 * 6 or square root of 144 and see the answer instantly.

📏 Unit conversions

Try 10 miles in km or 3 cups in liters for a quick, accurate conversion.

🌦️ Weather & time

Search weather tomorrow or time in Tokyo for an instant answer.

📖 Definitions

define:credible gives you a quick dictionary definition without leaving the search page.

Reading your results before you click

Once your results show up, take a moment to look them over before clicking. Each result usually shows a title, a short preview called a snippet, and the web address. Here's what to check:

🔎 Does the snippet match your question?

If the short preview text doesn't really relate to what you searched, skip it — even if the title sounds good.

🌐 Is the web address familiar?

A trusted site's address is usually short and simple, like nasa.gov. A confusing jumble of letters and numbers is a warning sign.

📢 Is it labeled "Sponsored"?

Ads appear at the very top and are labeled. They paid to be there — they aren't automatically the best or most trustworthy answer.

🖼️ Use the Images / News / Books tabs

Switch tabs above your results to search just pictures, news articles, books, or maps.

Two more handy tools

🕒 Tools → Time range

Click "Tools" under the search bar to see only recent results — perfect for news and things happening right now.

🔍 Reverse image search

Upload or paste a picture to find where else it's been used online — a great way to check if a photo is real.

💭 Think about it: A search result has a great title, but the snippet underneath doesn't answer your question at all. Should you click it?

Probably not — or at least not first! Try a couple of other results whose snippets actually match what you're looking for. A flashy title doesn't guarantee the page will be useful; the snippet is a much better preview of what you'll actually find.

Do

  • Use quotes for exact names or phrases
  • Check the Tools menu for date filters
  • Look at the web address before clicking
  • Skip past ads marked "Sponsored"

Don't

  • Type a whole question when a few words work
  • Trust the very first result without checking
  • Ignore the web address before clicking
  • Assume "first" always means "best"
